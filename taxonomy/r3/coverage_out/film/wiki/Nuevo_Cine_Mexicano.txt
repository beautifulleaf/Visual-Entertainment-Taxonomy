'''''Nuevo Cine Mexicano''''' ("New Mexican Cinema") is a Mexican film movement started in the early 1990s.<ref name=":0">{{Cite journal|last=Martin|first=Michael T.|year=2004|title=Mexican Cinema and the 'Generation of the 1990s'|journal=Framework: The Journal of Cinema and Media|volume=45|pages=115–128}}</ref> Filmmakers, critics, and scholars consider Nuevo Cine Mexicano a "rebirth" of Mexican cinema because of the production of higher-quality films. This rebirth led to high international praise as well as box-office success, unseen since the golden age of Mexican cinema of the 1930s to 1960s. The quality of Mexican films suffered in the decades following the golden age due in part to Mexican audiences watching more overseas films, especially Hollywood productions.<ref name=":3">{{Cite journal|last=Tsao|first=Leonardo Garcia|year=2007|title=After the Breakthrough of Amores Perros, What's next for Mexican Cinema?|journal=Film Comment|volume=37|pages=11–13}}</ref> This resulted in the rise of Mexican genres such as [[Luchador films]], ''[[Mexican sex comedy|sexicomedias]]'' and ultimately the low-budget direct-to-video [[Mexploitation]] film.<ref name=":2">{{Cite journal|last=Maciel|first=David|title=The Cinematic Renaissance of Contemporary Mexico 1985–1992|journal=UC Davis|pages=70–85}}</ref>

Many themes addressed in Nuevo Cine Mexicano include identity, tradition, and socio-political conflicts within Mexico itself.<ref name=":0" /> The movement has achieved international success with films such as director [[Alfonso Cuarón]]'s ''[[Y Tu Mamá También]]'' (2001), which was nominated for an  [[Academy Awards|Academy Award]] for Best Original Screenplay<ref>[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MdIWEAFyxd8 Pedro Almodóvar Wins Original Screenplay: 2003 Oscars]</ref> and at the [[Golden Globes]] for Best Foreign Film, and [[Alejandro González Iñárritu|Alejandro G. Iñárritu]]'s ''[[Amores Perros]]'' (2000), which was nominated for Best Foreign Film at the Academy Awards.<ref>[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9CwKWCz768 "Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon" Wins Foreign Language Film: 2001 Oscars]</ref> 

There is debate over when this "new wave" of Mexican cinema began and whether there are any clear parameters as to how it differs from other Mexican film movements other than "newfound audience enthusiasm".<ref name=":1">{{Cite journal|last=Hind|first=Emily|year=2004|title=Post-NAFTA Mexican Cinema 1998–2002|journal=Studies in Latin American Popular Culture|volume=23|pages=95–111}}</ref> Some cite the actual rejuvenation of Mexican cinema as starting in 1998 in a Post-NAFTA Mexico, beginning with the film ''[[Sexo, pudor y lágrimas]]'' (''Sex, Shame and Tears'').<ref name=":1" /> Others believe it began because of the international acclaim of the films such as ''[[Like Water for Chocolate (film)|Like Water for Chocolate]]'' (1992) and its nomination for Best Foreign Film at the Golden Globes.<ref>[https://www.goldenglobes.com/film/water-chocolate Like Water for Chocolate|Golden Globes]</ref><ref>[https://www.goldenglobes.com/winners-nominees/best-motion-picture-foreign-language?page=6 Best Motion Picture-Foreign Language|Page 7|Golden Globes]</ref> The definition of Nuevo Cine Mexicano also leads to the question, "What is a Mexican film?"—is it Mexican film because of who makes or stars in it, or because it takes place in Mexico?<ref name=":4">{{Cite book|title=Mex-Cine: Mexican Filmmaking, Production, and Consumption in the Twenty-first Century|last=Aldama|first=Frederick Luis|publisher=University of Michigan Press|year=2013|location=Michigan}}</ref>

==Origins==
[[File:0D3L0261_copia.jpg|thumb|right|200px|[[Alex García (producer)|Alex Garcia]], one of the most active producers of audiovisual content in the Spanish-speaking Americas.]]
The [[Golden Age of Mexican Cinema]] occurred from the 1930s to the 1960s, afterward, a period of low-budget [[B-movies]] funded by the state of Mexico was the primary source for films for the Mexican public.<ref name=":3" /> A resurgence of Mexican cinema was believed to occur in the 1970s, however, its success was short-lived as the majority Mexican filmgoers preferred Hollywood films.<ref name=":2" /><ref name=":1" />

Before the 1990s, the Mexican film industry was primarily funded by the state in coordination with the [[Instituto Mexicano de Cinematografía]] (Mexican Film Institute, IMCINE). There was a decrease in Mexican audiences watching Mexican-produced films in favor of Hollywood blockbusters as well as "film production dropp[ing] to an all-time low" due to the economic crash in 1994.<ref name=":3" /> The IMCINE produced roughly five films a year during the crisis.<ref name=":0" /><ref name=":4" /> The main influx of directors and filmmakers, as well as funding, primarily came from the IMCINE. The incoming filmmakers, nicknamed the "1990s Generation", were helped by the generation of 1968 with their filmmaking skills.<ref name=":2" />

One of the most successful filmmakers of the 1990s Generation of Mexican filmmakers, [[Guillermo del Toro]], said that "In the 80s there was a huge void in Mexican cinema, then my generation picked up the staff in the early 90s."<ref>{{Cite news|title=The New Migration – "Y Tu Mama" Is Just the Latest in a Trend of Quality Mexican Films Traversing the Rio Grande|last=Garcia|first=Chris|date=5 April 2002|work=Austin American Statesman}}</ref> However, during the 1970s "technical experimentation" took precedence within the film community, and through the 1980s films "catered to the lowest common denominator", the 1990s Generation learned by working together with the filmmakers of the late 60s and 70s.<ref name=":0" />

==Themes==
[[File:Ripstein-2.jpg|thumb|left|200px|[[Arturo Ripstein]] in 1997, Ripstein won the [[National Prize of Arts and Sciences (Mexico)|National Prize of Arts and Sciences]], the second filmmaker after Buñuel to do so.]]

Social divisions within Mexico is a reoccurring theme within Nuevo Cine Mexicano, including the films ''Y Tu Mamá También, El crimen del Padre Amaro'' (2002), and ''Amores Perros.''

[[File:Director_Mariana_Chenillo.jpg|thumb|right|200px|[[Mariana Chenillo]] became the first female director to win an [[Ariel Award]] for [[Ariel Award for Best Picture|Best Picture]] back in 2010.]]

Though the films touch on the "socio-geographic divisions" of Mexico in different ways.<ref name=":1" /> In ''[[Amores Perros]]'', the economic divisions are portrayed through the differences between the main characters' homes. El Chivo lives in a "seedy residence" which that is juxtaposed with his daughter's "respectable home".<ref name=":1" /> With ''[[El crimen del padre Amaro]]'' (''The Crime of Father Amaro'') the traditions of the Catholic Church, which remains a prominent influence in Mexico, is questioned when a young priest has sex with a teenager leading to her death from an abortion.<ref name=":1" /> Other subjects such as homosexuality and political corruption are briefly touched on in ''[[Y Tu Mamá También]]'' (''And Your Mother Too''), helping to set up a background of what Mexico is and is not.<ref name=":5">{{Cite journal|last=Menne|first=Jeff|year=2007|title=A Mexican Nouvelle Vague: The Logic of New Waves Under Globalization|journal=Cinema Journal|volume=47|pages=70–92|doi=10.1353/cj.2007.0054 }}</ref>  The two main male characters in the film differ in their social standings because of their families' political connections. What ultimately breaks their friendship apart is having sex with one another.<ref name=":5" /> Within Nuevo Cine Mexicano, filmmakers try to portray such social and economic troubles within Mexico through different perspectives, which commonly goes against the sometimes stereotypical portrayals of Mexico and its inhabitants in U.S. and European films.

The characterizations of Europeans or foreigners, specifically Spaniards, are relatively negative. In several works in Nuevo Cine Mexicano, the conflict within the story is due to a person of Spanish descent. Either the non-foreigners in the film who associate with the Spaniards are drastically changed or the Spaniards themselves meet a tragic end.<ref name=":1" /> The filmmakers use this trope to recall Mexico's past, specifically with Spain's colonization of Mexico.<ref name=":1" /> The style of the films generally mimics the "art house" films of previous decades, since the state of Mexico had the greatest authority over the production of movies.<ref name=":5" /> Directors specifically adopted this style to move away from the state and into independent productions, which a majority of Nuevo Cine Mexicano is.<ref name=":2" /> Production studios normally fund one to two million dollars per film, due to the lack of mainstream production.<ref name=":4" /> The influence of "[[North American Free Trade Agreement|NAFTAtrade]] and tax policies" made it harder for the public to fund such productions.<ref name=":4" /> Women are statistically underrepresented in creative positions like the called "[[celluloid ceiling]]" of the underrepresentation of women in hiring and employment in Hollywood. 

[[File:Diego_Luna.jpg|thumb|left|175px|[[Diego Luna]] actor, director, and producer.]]

However it has been improving; two of the most popular films ''El secreto de Romeila'' (1988) directed by [[Busi Cortés]] and ''Los pasos de Ana'' (1990) by Marisa Sistach are considered the doors that opened opportunity for women filmmakers in Mexico as well as created a new genre that people were not familiar with, labeled as 'women's cinema'.<ref name=":5" /> The phenomenal growth of 'women's cinema', not only meant that there would be an infinite expansion in the list of female names as filmmakers or creators; in reality, it created a daunting cinematic genre by objectifying women as well as displacing them within the film industry.<ref name=":5" /> According to Patricia Torres San Martín, an honorable film scholar, there is a new theme emerging within the film industry in Mexico which is known as the 'new female identity'.<ref name=":5" /> [[Yalitza Aparicio]] debut performance in ''[[Roma (2018 film)|Roma]]'' (2018) makes her the first [[Indigenous peoples of the Americas|Indigenous Latin American]] ([[Indigenous peoples of Mexico|Indigenous Mexican]]) to be nominated in any category at the Academy Awards.

''[[Museum (2018 film)|Museum]]'' won the [[Silver Bear for Best Screenplay|best script]] award at the [[Berlin International Film Festival]]. The film tells the story of the famous robbery to the [[National Museum of Anthropology (Mexico)|National Museum of Anthropology]] on 25 December 1985, in Mexico City.

''[[Presumed Guilty (film)|Presumed Guilty]]'' is a documentary following Antonio Zúñiga, who was falsely convicted of murder. It holds the box office record for documentary in Mexico. According to ''[[The Economist]]'', this is "by far the most successful documentary in Mexico's history."

==Main players==
[[File:Fernando Eimbcke.jpg|thumb|120px|[[Fernando Eimbcke]] won the [[Cartagena Film Festival]] in 2009.]]
[[File:Eiza Gonzalez (cropped).jpg|thumb|upright|left|120px|[[Eiza Gonzalez]] in 2015.]]
[[Guillermo del Toro]], [[Alfonso Cuarón]], and [[Alejandro González Iñárritu]] make up the "Three Amigos", the main Mexican film directors of Nuevo Cine Mexicano. All have created films produced in Mexico and Hollywood. Critics and award shows consider these three as the premier directors in their craft.<ref>[https://www.nbcnews.com/storyline/oscars/how-mexicans-became-hollywoods-best-directors-n310746 How Mexicans Became Hollywood's Best Directors-NBC News]</ref> Each produces and uses actors and cinematographers from Mexico, even in their Hollywood made productions.<ref name=":4" /> "Poster-boy" actors [[Gael García Bernal]] and [[Diego Luna]] have also moved on to several Hollywood productions, yet their popularity in Mexican cinema has proven to endure throughout the years.<ref name=":4" /> Other directors include [[Alonso Ruizpalacios]], [[Fernando Eimbcke]], [[Jonás Cuarón]], [[Issa López]] and [[Carlos López Estrada]] other actors include [[Eugenio Derbez]], [[Eiza González]], [[Diego Boneta]], [[Tenoch Huerta]], [[Kate del Castillo]], [[Marina de Tavira]], [[Diego Calva]], [[Daniel Giménez Cacho]], [[Omar Chaparro]], [[Ana de la Reguera]], [[José María Yazpik]], [[Martha Higareda]], [[Adriana Paz]], [[Salma Hayek]], [[Marco Pérez (actor)|Marco Perez]], [[Adriana Barraza]], [[Damián Alcázar]], [[Irene Azuela]], [[Luis Gerardo Méndez]], [[Eduardo Verástegui]], [[Karla Souza]], [[Ana Claudia Talancón]], [[Juan Daniel García Treviño]], [[Joaquín Cosío]], [[Alfonso Herrera]], [[Yalitza Aparicio]], [[Ilse Salas]], [[Demian Bichir]], [[Tony Dalton]], [[Cecilia Suárez]], [[Cassandra Ciangherotti]] and [[Jaime Camil]].

==European influence==
Since 2000, some directors have made "independent productions looking for more personal expression", under a greater influence of [[European cinema]].<ref>{{cite book | author= González Vargas, Carla| title= Rutas del cine mexicano | publisher= CONACULTA IMCINE | year=2006| isbn=9685893292|display-authors=etal}}</ref> The most representative films of this trend<ref>{{Cite book |last=Wood |first=Jason |url=https://books.google.com/books/about/Talking_Movies.html?id=F4X-lX3kLGAC |title=Talking Movies: Contemporary World Filmmakers in Interview |date=2006 |publisher=Wallflower Press |isbn=978-1-904764-90-8 |pages=189 |language=en}}</ref> are the films of [[Carlos Reygadas]] (''[[Japón]]'' and ''[[Batalla en el cielo|Battle in Heaven]]''), whose use of non-professional actors was influenced by [[Italian neorealism]]. Other films include: ''[[A Thousand Clouds of Peace Fence the Sky, Love; Your Being Love Will Never End|Mil nubes de paz cercan el cielo, amor, jamás acabarás de ser amor]]'' (''A Thousand Clouds of Peace Fence the Sky, Love; Your Being Love Will Never End'') and ''[[Broken Sky (film)|El cielo dividido]]'' (''Broken Sky''), directed by [[Julián Hernández (filmmaker)|Julián Hernández]], and ''[[Sangre (film)|Sangre]]'', directed by [[Amat Escalante]] and produced by Jaime Romandía and Reygadas.
