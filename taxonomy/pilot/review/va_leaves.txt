
## A Painting — a picture made by laying paint or ink on a movable flat support — paper, vellum, panel, canvas, cloth or silk. Wall painting fixed to a building is L.
### A1 Manuscript & Album Painting
A1-1 Arab Painting: manuscript painting of the Arab lands from the 12th to the 15th century: illustrated scientific texts, the Maqamat of al-Hariri and Kalila wa Dimna, made in Baghdad, Mosul, Syria and Mamluk Egypt (al-Wasiti's 1237 Maqamat). [aka Arabic miniature; Baghdad school; Mesopotamian school]
A1-2 Persian Miniature: Persian manuscript and album painting from the Mongol conquest to the 19th century, taught in court workshops whose schools are named after their centres (Herat, Tabriz, Qazvin, Isfahan, Shiraz, Bukhara); kinds are those schools and their masters (Bihzad, Sultan Muhammad, Riza-yi 'Abbasi). [aka Persian painting; Iranian miniature; negārgari]
A1-3 Ottoman Miniature: painting of the Ottoman court workshop (nakkaşhane) for histories, festival books and albums, from Mehmed II to the 18th century (Matrakçı Nasuh's topographies, Nakkaş Osman's Hünername, Levni's Surname-i Vehbi). [aka Turkish miniature; nakış]
A1-4 Mughal Painting: painting of the Mughal court atelier and its offshoots in South Asia, 16th–19th century: the Hamzanama and the illustrated histories of Akbar, Jahangir's portraits and natural history, and the sub-imperial and provincial schools (Lucknow, Murshidabad). [aka Mughal miniature; Indo-Persian painting]
A1-5 Deccan Painting: painting of the Deccan sultanates (Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda) and of Hyderabad after them, 16th–19th century, with its lyrical colour and Persian, Turkman and Vijayanagara sources. [aka Deccani painting; Deccani miniature]
A1-6 Company Painting: paintings made by Indian artists for officials of the East India Company and other Europeans, 18th–19th century, in a hybrid of Mughal technique and European natural history and topography (Delhi, Lucknow, Patna, Murshidabad, Tanjore). [aka Company school; Company style]
### A5 Easel Painting
A5-1 Qajar Painting: court painting of Qajar Iran (1789–1925) in oil on canvas and in watercolour: life-size royal portraits and figures of pleasure (Mihr 'Ali), and the realist watercolours of Abu'l-Hasan Ghaffari (Sani' al-Mulk). [aka Qajar art (painting)]

## B Drawings & Prints — a drawing made as an end in itself or as a design, or an impression pulled from a matrix (woodblock, plate, stone, screen).
### B1 Drawing
B1-1 Saz Style: the Ottoman court style of feathery serrated leaves, rosettes and fabulous creatures drawn in ink by the nakkaşhane under Shahkulu and Kara Memi (16th century), and carried from drawings into tiles, textiles and bindings. [aka saz yolu; saz leaf style]

## C Book Arts & Calligraphy — the written or decorated book or page is the work: calligraphy, illumination (ornament), binding and paper decoration, or a manuscript tradition studied as the book.
### C1 Calligraphy
C1-1 Kufic: the angular early Arabic script of Qur'ans on parchment and of inscriptions, with its later Eastern (broken cursive), floriated, plaited and square (banna'i) forms. [aka Kufi; Kufic script]
C1-2 Maghribi Script: the rounded scripts of al-Andalus, North Africa and West Africa (mabsut, mujawhar, Andalusi, Sudani), written with a pointed pen. [aka Maghrebi script; Andalusi script]
C1-3 Six Pens (al-Aqlam al-Sitta): the proportioned cursive scripts — thuluth, naskh, muhaqqaq, rayhani, tawqi' and riqa' — codified by Ibn Muqla and Ibn al-Bawwab and perfected by Yaqut al-Musta'simi, then carried by his pupils and by the Ottoman masters (Sheikh Hamdullah, Hafiz Osman). [aka aqlam al-sitta; six scripts; Thuluth; Naskh; Muhaqqaq]
C1-4 Nasta'liq: the hanging Persian script attributed to Mir 'Ali Tabrizi (14th century), the script of Persian poetry and album calligraphy (Sultan 'Ali Mashhadi, Mir 'Ali Haravi, Mir 'Imad), with its shikasta (broken) offshoot. [aka Nastaliq; nastaʿlīq; ta'liq; shikasta]
C1-5 Diwani: the Ottoman chancery script of decrees and imperial documents, with the tughra, the sultan's calligraphic monogram. [aka Divani; diwani jali; tughra]
C1-6 Sini: Chinese Arabic calligraphy, written with a brush in a rounded style on scrolls and Qur'ans of the Hui Muslims. [aka Sini script; Chinese Arabic calligraphy]
### C2 Illuminated Manuscripts & Illumination
C2-1 Early Qur'anic Manuscripts: Qur'ans written on parchment in Hijazi and Kufic scripts from the 7th to the 10th century, in horizontal format with sparse illuminated headings (the Blue Qur'an, the Tübingen and Birmingham fragments). [aka early Qur'ans; Hijazi Qur'ans; Kufic Qur'ans]
C2-2 Islamic Illumination: the illumination of Qur'ans and books after about 1000: gilded and painted frontispieces, sura headings, margins and borders, in the Abbasid, Mamluk, Ilkhanid, Timurid and Safavid (tazhib), Ottoman (tezhip) and Mughal traditions, with its named illuminators. [aka tazhib; tezhip; Qur'an illumination]
C2-3 Timbuktu Manuscripts: the manuscripts of the West African Islamic scholarly tradition (Timbuktu, Djenné, the Sahel), in Arabic and in Ajami (African languages in Arabic script), with their Sudani script and leather satchel bindings. [aka West African manuscripts; Ajami manuscripts]
C2-4 Malay Manuscripts: illuminated Malay manuscripts in Jawi script from the Islamic sultanates of the Malay world (Aceh, Terengganu, Java), with their floral frontispieces. [aka Jawi manuscripts; Malay Qur'ans]
### C3 Bookbinding
C3-1 Islamic Bookbinding: the bindings of the Islamic book: tooled, stamped and filigree-cut leather with the envelope flap, and from the 15th century painted lacquer bindings. [aka Islamic book covers]
### C4 Paper Arts
C4-1 Ebru: paper marbling as practised in Ottoman Turkey and Iran: colours floated on size and transferred to paper, used for album margins and endpapers. [aka Turkish marbling; abri; paper marbling]

## D Sculpture & Carving — carved, modelled or cast in three dimensions as an image or as carving for its own sake: statues, reliefs, carved stucco and stone ornament, ivory, bone, jade, rock crystal and hardstone, masks and puppets.
### D1 Architectural Carving & Stucco
D1-1 Samarra Stucco: the carved and moulded stucco of the Abbasid capital Samarra (9th century) in its three styles, of which the third, the bevelled style, carried abstract ornament across the early Islamic world. [aka bevelled style; Samarra styles A–C]
D1-2 Nasrid Stucco (Yesería): the carved and painted stucco of Nasrid Granada and the Mudéjar workshops (Alhambra wall panels, muqarnas). [aka yesería; Alhambra stucco]
### D2 Ivory & Bone Carving
D2-1 Andalusian Ivories: carved ivory boxes and pyxides of the Umayyad caliphal workshops of Córdoba and Madinat al-Zahra and of the taifa courts (10th–11th century). [aka Caliphal ivories; ivories of al-Andalus]
D2-2 Siculo-Arabic Ivories: painted and gilded ivory caskets made in Norman Sicily and southern Italy (12th–13th century) in an Arabic decorative idiom. [aka Siculo-Arabic ivory caskets]
### D3 Hardstone, Rock Crystal & Jade Carving
D3-1 Fatimid Rock Crystal: vessels and objects carved from rock crystal in Fatimid Egypt (10th–11th century), above all the ewers with relief decoration later mounted in European church treasuries. [aka Fatimid rock crystal ewers]
D3-2 Mughal Jade: jade and hardstone vessels carved for the Timurid and Mughal courts, culminating in Shah Jahan's wine cup (1657). [aka Mughal hardstone carving]
### D4 Masks & Puppets
D4-1 Karagöz Shadow Puppets: the translucent painted leather puppets of the Ottoman shadow theatre of Karagöz and Hacivat. [aka Karagöz and Hacivat; hayal-i zıll]

## E Ceramics — made of fired clay: pottery, tiles and ceramic objects, placed by their body (fabric) and then their named ware.
### E1 Earthenware
E1-1 Abbasid Tin-Glazed Ware: earthenware with an opaque white tin glaze made in Basra and southern Iraq from the 9th century in imitation of Chinese porcelain, painted in cobalt blue and green (Abbasid blue-and-white). [aka opaque white ware; Basra ware; Abbasid blue-and-white]
E1-2 Abbasid Lustreware: the first lustre pottery: tin-glazed earthenware painted in metallic lustre in 9th–10th-century Iraq, polychrome then monochrome, exported as far as Samarra and Kairouan. [aka Iraqi lustreware; Samarra lustre]
E1-3 Fatimid Lustreware: lustre-painted earthenware of Fatimid Egypt (10th–12th century), often figural, signed by potters such as Muslim ibn al-Dahhan and Sa'd. [aka Egyptian lustreware]
E1-4 Samanid Slipware: earthenware of Nishapur and Samarqand (9th–10th century) painted in coloured slips under a clear glaze, above all the epigraphic ware with Arabic aphorisms on a white ground. [aka Nishapur ware; epigraphic slipware; Samanid epigraphic ware]
E1-5 Splashware: earthenware splashed with green, yellow and brown glazes in imitation of Chinese sancai, made in Iraq and Iran from the 9th century. [aka splashed ware; Islamic sancai imitations]
E1-6 Persian Sgraffito Ware: slip-covered earthenware incised through the slip and glazed, made in Iran and the Caucasus from the 10th to the 13th century (Garrus, Aghkand and Amol wares). [aka Garrus ware; Aghkand ware; Amol ware; sgraffito]
E1-7 Islamic Unglazed Pottery: unglazed earthenware with carved, moulded, stamped or barbotine decoration, above all the pierced filters of water jugs (Egypt, Syria, Iraq, 10th–14th century). [aka jug filters; barbotine ware]
E1-8 Hispano-Moresque Ware: tin-glazed and lustred earthenware of Islamic and Christian Spain (Málaga, Manises, Paterna, Valencia), 13th–16th century, carried to Italy as the model of maiolica. [aka Valencian lustreware; Manises ware; Málaga ware]
E1-9 Çanakkale Pottery: the boldly modelled and splash-painted earthenware of Çanakkale on the Dardanelles (17th–19th century). [aka Chanak ware; Çanakkale ceramics]
E1-10 Moroccan Pottery: the urban and rural earthenware of Morocco: the blue-and-polychrome tin-glazed wares of Fez, the pottery of Safi and the green glazed ware of Tamegroute. [aka Fez pottery; Safi pottery; Tamegroute pottery]
E1-11 Kabyle Pottery: hand-built earthenware painted in red and black slips by Kabyle women potters of northern Algeria. [aka Berber pottery; Amazigh pottery]
E1-12 Sejnane Pottery: hand-modelled earthenware of the women of Sejnane in northern Tunisia, inscribed by UNESCO in 2018. [aka pottery skills of the women of Sejnane]
E1-13 Rishtan Ceramics: the blue-glazed (ishkor) earthenware of Rishtan and the centres of the Fergana Valley and Bukhara oasis (Gijduvan) in Uzbekistan. [aka Uzbek ceramics; Gijduvan ceramics]
### E2 Fritware (Stonepaste)
E2-1 Seljuk Pottery: fritware of Seljuq Iran and its neighbours (11th–13th century) other than its named lustre, mina'i and lajvardina wares: monochrome and carved, silhouette, laqabi and underglaze-painted wares. [aka Seljuq pottery; monochrome ware; silhouette ware; laqabi ware]
E2-2 Kashan Lustreware: lustre-painted fritware of Kashan (late 12th–14th century) — vessels and the star, cross and mihrab tiles of Iranian shrines — signed by potter families such as Abu Tahir and Abu Zayd. [aka Kashan ware; Persian lustreware]
E2-3 Mina'i Ware: overglaze-painted fritware of Kashan (late 12th–early 13th century), with figural scenes in seven colours. [aka haft-rangi ware]
E2-4 Lajvardina: Ilkhanid fritware with a cobalt-blue (lajvard) or turquoise glaze overpainted in white, red and gold leaf (late 13th–14th century). [aka Lajvard ware]
E2-5 Raqqa Ware: fritware of Raqqa and Syria (12th–13th century), lustred or underglaze-painted in black under a turquoise glaze. [aka Raqqa pottery; Syrian fritware]
E2-6 Sultanabad Ware: Ilkhanid fritware of the late 13th–14th century with a grey-green slip and Chinese-inspired lotus and animal designs. [aka Sultanabad pottery]
E2-7 Iznik Pottery: the fritware of Iznik in Ottoman Anatolia (late 15th–17th century) — vessels and tiles in its successive styles: blue-and-white (Abraham of Kütahya), Golden Horn, Damascus and Rhodian with its bole red. [aka Iznik ware; Iznik tiles; Rhodian ware]
E2-8 Damascus Ware: Ottoman-period fritware and tiles made in Damascus (16th–17th century), painted in blues, green and manganese without Iznik's red. [aka Damascus tiles; Syrian Ottoman pottery]
E2-9 Kubachi Ware: Safavid fritware of north-western Iran (16th–17th century) with crackled glaze, found in the village of Kubachi in Daghestan. [aka Kubachi pottery]
E2-10 Safavid Pottery: the fritware of Safavid Iran (16th–17th century) other than Kubachi ware: Kirman and Mashhad blue-and-white in the manner of Chinese porcelain, celadon imitations and late lustre. [aka Kirman ware; Persian blue-and-white; Gombroon ware]
E2-11 Kütahya Pottery: the fritware of Kütahya in western Anatolia (18th–19th century), small vessels and tiles often made by Armenian potters for church and household. [aka Kutahya ware]
E2-12 Qajar Ceramics: the tiles and vessels of Qajar Iran (19th century): moulded and painted figural tiles of Tehran and Shiraz (Ali Muhammad Isfahani) and revival lustre. [aka Qajar tiles]

## F Glass — made of glass, placed by how it is formed and decorated.
### F1 Blown & Moulded Glass
F1-1 Beykoz Glass: Ottoman glass of the Beykoz and Istanbul workshops (late 18th–19th century), above all the çeşm-i bülbül ('nightingale's eye') ware with spiral white and coloured threads. [aka çeşm-i bülbül]
F1-2 Syrian Glassblowing: the free-blown glass of the Damascus and Hebron workshops, inscribed by UNESCO in 2023 as a heritage in need of urgent safeguarding. [aka Damascus glass; Hebron glass]
### F2 Cut & Carved Glass
F2-1 Islamic Cut Glass: glass decorated by wheel-cutting and relief-carving in Iran, Iraq and Egypt (9th–11th century), from facet-cut bowls to relief-cut cameo glass and the Hedwig glasses. [aka relief-cut glass; Hedwig glasses]
### F3 Enamelled & Gilded Glass
F3-1 Mamluk Enamelled Glass: enamelled and gilded glass of Ayyubid and Mamluk Syria and Egypt (13th–14th century): mosque lamps, beakers and bottles with blazons and inscriptions. [aka Ayyubid-Mamluk enamelled glass; mosque lamps]
### F4 Lustre-Painted Glass
F4-1 Islamic Lustre-Painted Glass: glass stained with metallic lustre in Egypt, Syria and Iraq (8th–11th century), the predecessor of lustre pottery. [aka lustre-stained glass]

## G Metalwork, Arms & Jewellery — made of metal: vessels and objects, instruments, arms and armour, and jewellery.
### G1 Metalwork
G1-1 Khurasan Metalwork: cast and inlaid brass of eastern Iran and Herat (12th–13th century), with silver and copper inlay and figural bands (the Bobrinsky Bucket, 1163). [aka Herat metalwork; Seljuq inlaid metalwork]
G1-2 Mosul School of Metalwork: inlaid brass made by craftsmen with the nisba al-Mawsili in Mosul, Syria and Egypt (13th century), the model for Ayyubid and Mamluk inlay. [aka Mosul metalwork; al-Mawsili inlay]
G1-3 Mamluk Metalwork: inlaid brass and bronze of Mamluk Egypt and Syria (1250–1517) — basins, candlesticks, Qur'an boxes — with epigraphic blazons (the Baptistère de Saint Louis by Muhammad ibn al-Zayn). [aka Mamluk inlaid brass]
G1-4 Venetian-Saracenic Metalwork: inlaid brass made in the Islamic manner in the 15th–16th century and long thought Venetian, now mostly given to Mamluk and Iranian workshops. [aka Veneto-Saracenic metalwork]
G1-5 Ottoman Tombak: fire-gilded copper of the Ottoman court (17th–19th century): ewers, basins, censers and mosque fittings. [aka tombak; gilt copper]
G1-6 Bidri Ware: zinc-alloy objects inlaid with silver and blackened, from Bidar in the Deccan (from the 14th century). [aka Bidriware]
G1-7 Koftgari: damascening of steel with gold and silver wire, practised in Mughal and Rajput India and in Iran for arms and objects. [aka damascening; tah-nishan]
G1-8 Islamic Astrolabes: brass astrolabes and celestial globes of the Islamic world, engraved instruments made by named makers in Baghdad, Isfahan, Lahore and al-Andalus. [aka astrolabes; celestial globes]
G1-9 Firoozeh Koobi: Iranian metal objects set with turquoise chips in a mosaic over the surface (Isfahan, Mashhad). [aka turquoise inlay]
### G2 Arms & Armour
G2-1 Islamic Arms and Armour: the arms and armour of the Islamic courts: Mamluk and Ottoman helmets and mail, Persian shamshirs and watered-steel (wootz) blades, Ottoman yatağans and Mughal talwars, katars and jade-hilted daggers. [aka shamshir; yatağan; talwar; wootz steel; Damascus steel]
### G3 Jewellery
G3-1 Fatimid Jewellery: gold jewellery of Fatimid Egypt and Syria (10th–12th century) in filigree and granulation, often enamelled. [aka Fatimid gold filigree]
G3-2 Mughal Jewellery: jewellery and jewelled objects of the Mughal court: kundan setting, enamelled (minakari) backs, carved emeralds and jade inlaid with gems. [aka kundan; minakari; meenakari]
G3-3 Bedouin Jewellery: the silver jewellery of the Bedouin and settled peoples of Arabia, Yemen and Oman, worked in filigree, granulation and with coral and carnelian. [aka Yemeni silver; Omani silver]
G3-4 Berber Jewellery: the silver jewellery of the Amazigh peoples of Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia: fibulae, headdresses and the cloisonné enamel of Kabylia. [aka Amazigh jewellery; Kabyle jewellery]

## H Textiles, Carpets & Dress — made of fibre: carpets and rugs, woven, embroidered, dyed and felted textiles, and dress.
### H1 Pile Carpets & Rugs
H1-1 Persian Carpet: knotted-pile carpets of Iran, from the Safavid court carpets (the Ardabil carpet, hunting and vase carpets, Polonaise carpets) to the city, village and tribal rugs named by their centres and peoples (Tabriz, Kashan, Isfahan, Kerman, Heriz, Qashqai, Bakhtiari). [aka Persian rug; Iranian carpet; farsh]
H1-2 Anatolian Rug: knotted-pile carpets of Anatolia from the Seljuq Konya carpets onward: animal carpets, the 'Holbein' and 'Lotto' carpets known from European paintings, Ushak star and medallion carpets, and the village rugs of Bergama, Konya and Ghiordes. [aka Turkish carpet; Ushak carpet; Holbein carpet; Lotto carpet]
H1-3 Mamluk Carpet: carpets woven in Cairo under the Mamluks (late 15th–early 16th century) in red, green and blue with kaleidoscopic geometric fields. [aka Cairene carpet]
H1-4 Caucasian Carpet: knotted-pile rugs of the Caucasus — Kazak, Karabagh, Shirvan, Kuba and the dragon carpets — and of the Azerbaijani schools. [aka Caucasian rug; Azerbaijani carpet]
H1-5 Turkmen Carpet: knotted-pile carpets and tent furnishings of the Turkmen tribes (Tekke, Yomut, Salor, Saryk, Ersari), with their tribal güls. [aka Turkoman carpet; Bukhara rug]
H1-6 Baluch Rug: dark-toned knotted rugs of the Baluch peoples of eastern Iran, Afghanistan and Pakistan. [aka Baluchi rug; Belouch]
H1-7 Afghan Rug: knotted rugs of Afghanistan, including the war rugs woven since 1979 with weapons and maps. [aka war rugs; chobi rugs]
H1-8 Mughal Carpet: knotted carpets of the Mughal workshops of Lahore, Agra and Kashmir (late 16th–17th century): pictorial and flowering-plant carpets of very fine weave. [aka Indo-Persian carpet; Indo-Isfahan carpet]
H1-9 Moroccan Rug: the pile and flatwoven rugs of the Amazigh tribes of Morocco (Beni Ouarain, Azilal, Boucherouite) and of the urban workshops of Rabat. [aka Berber carpet; Amazigh rug]
### H2 Flatweaves
H2-1 Kilim: tapestry-woven flatweaves of Anatolia, Iran, the Caucasus, Central Asia and North Africa, from slit-tapestry Anatolian kilims to Persian gelims, gabbeh-like tribal weaves and Tunisian mergoum. [aka kelim; gelim; mergoum]
H2-2 Soumak: weft-wrapped flatweaves of the Caucasus and north-west Iran, with a herringbone surface. [aka sumak]
H2-3 Al Sadu: the warp-faced weaving of the Bedouin of Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar, for tents and camel trappings (UNESCO 2011). [aka Sadu weaving; Bedouin weaving]
### H3 Woven Silks & Figured Textiles
H3-1 Tiraz: inscribed textiles from the caliphal workshops (tiraz) of the Umayyads, Abbasids and Fatimids, with bands of embroidered or tapestry-woven Arabic inscriptions. [aka tiraz textiles; tiraz bands]
H3-2 Safavid Silk Textiles: the figured silks, lampas, brocades and velvets of Safavid Iran (Ghiyath al-Din 'Ali-yi Naqshband), with figural scenes and floral fields. [aka Persian velvet; Safavid brocade]
H3-3 Ottoman Silk Textiles: the silk lampas (kemha) and velvets (çatma) of Bursa and Istanbul (15th–17th century), with ogival lattices, tulips and çintamani. [aka kemha; çatma; Bursa silk]
H3-4 Nasrid Silk: silks woven in Nasrid Granada and Almería (13th–15th century), with interlace, inscriptions and the 'Alhambra silks'. [aka Alhambra silks; Hispano-Islamic silk]
H3-5 Termeh: Persian tapestry-twill woven with the boteh, made in Yazd and Kerman. [aka Persian termeh; Yazd brocade]
H3-6 Kashmir Shawl: twill-tapestry shawls woven from pashmina in Kashmir (17th–19th century), whose boteh became the paisley of European imitations. [aka Kani shawl; pashmina shawl]
### H4 Embroidery & Needlework
H4-1 Suzani: large embroidered hangings and covers of Uzbekistan and Tajikistan (Bukhara, Nurata, Samarkand, Shakhrisabz), worked in silk chain and couching stitch. [aka suzani embroidery]
H4-2 Ottoman Embroidery: the silk and metal-thread embroidery of Ottoman households and court: towels, napkins, covers and garments, worked in double running and satin stitch. [aka Turkish embroidery]
H4-3 Palestinian Embroidery: tatreez: the cross-stitch embroidery of Palestinian women's dress by village and region (UNESCO 2021). [aka tatreez]
H4-4 Pateh: the wool embroidery of Kerman in Iran, densely covering woollen cloth with boteh and floral designs. [aka Kerman embroidery]
H4-5 Rasht Embroidery: felt appliqué and chain-stitch embroidery of Rasht in Gilan (Iran). [aka Rashti-duzi]
H4-6 Oya: Turkish needle lace: knotted borders of flowers for headscarves. [aka oya lace; Turkish needle lace]
H4-7 Khayamiya: the appliqué tent panels of the Cairo tentmakers' street, Sharia al-Khayamiya. [aka Egyptian appliqué]
H4-8 Balochi Needlework: the dense geometric embroidery of Baloch women's dress (Iran, Pakistan, Afghanistan). [aka Baloch embroidery]
### H5 Dyed & Printed Textiles
H5-1 Central Asian Ikat: warp-ikat silks and silk-cotton (adras, atlas) of Bukhara, Samarkand and the Fergana Valley (abrband, 'cloud-binding'). [aka abrband; adras; atlas]
H5-2 Qalamkar: block-printed and painted cotton of Isfahan. [aka ghalamkar; Persian qalamkari]
### H6 Felt
H6-1 Kyrgyz and Kazakh Felt: the mosaic felt carpets (shyrdak), pressed felt (ala-kiyiz, tus kiiz) and embroidered felt of the Kyrgyz and Kazakh (UNESCO 2012). [aka shyrdak; ala-kiyiz; tus kiiz]
### H7 Dress & Costume
H7-1 Palestinian Costume: the embroidered thobes and headdresses of Palestinian women, distinct by village and region. [aka thobe; Palestinian dress]
H7-2 Ottoman Court Dress: the kaftans and garments of the Ottoman sultans and court, kept in the Topkapı Palace treasury. [aka Ottoman kaftan]

## I Woodwork, Lacquer & Furniture — made of wood, or of lacquer and papier-mâché: carved and inlaid woodwork, lacquerware and furniture.
### I1 Carved & Inlaid Woodwork
I1-1 Mashrabiya: screens of turned wooden spindles in latticework, for windows and balconies of Cairo and the Arab world. [aka mashrabiyya; shanashil; rawshan]
I1-2 Khatam: Persian micro-marquetry of wood, bone and metal assembled in geometric patterns (Shiraz, Isfahan). [aka khatam-kari; Persian marquetry]
I1-3 Ottoman Mother-of-Pearl Inlay: Ottoman woodwork inlaid with mother-of-pearl, tortoiseshell and ivory (sedefkârlık): Qur'an stands, chests and thrones. [aka sedef kakma; sedefkârlık]
I1-4 Palestinian Olive-Wood Carving: carved olive wood of Bethlehem and Jerusalem, devotional and souvenir objects since the 16th century. [aka Bethlehem olive wood]
### I2 Lacquer
I2-1 Persian Lacquerwork: painted and varnished papier-mâché and wood of Iran — pen boxes, mirror cases, bindings and caskets — from the Timurid and Safavid periods to its Zand and Qajar flowering (Muhammad Zaman, 'Ali Ashraf, Muhammad Isma'il). [aka Persian lacquer; lacquer painting]
I2-2 Kashmiri Papier-Mâché: painted and varnished papier-mâché of Kashmir (boxes, vases), with floral and hunting designs. [aka kar-e-kalamdani]

## J Photography — an image made with a camera. Built at scale (artist-led: photographers under schools and movements).

## K Architecture — a building or designed built whole, placed by its style; buildings are representative works and architects are kinds.
### K1 Islamic Architecture
K1-1 Umayyad Architecture: the architecture of the Umayyad caliphate (661–750): the Dome of the Rock, the Great Mosque of Damascus and the desert palaces (Qusayr 'Amra, Khirbat al-Mafjar, Mshatta). [aka ]
K1-2 Abbasid Architecture: the architecture of the Abbasid caliphate (750–1258): Samarra's mosques and palaces, the round city of Baghdad and the Tulunid mosque of Cairo. [aka ]
K1-3 Fatimid Architecture: the architecture of the Fatimid caliphate in Ifriqiya and Egypt (909–1171): al-Azhar, al-Hakim and the gates of Cairo. [aka ]
K1-4 Moorish Architecture: the Islamic architecture of al-Andalus and the Maghreb: the caliphal Great Mosque of Córdoba and Madinat al-Zahra, the taifa Aljafería, Almoravid and Almohad mosques and the Nasrid Alhambra. [aka Andalusian architecture; Hispano-Moorish architecture]
K1-5 Mudéjar Architecture: the Islamic-derived architecture built under Christian rule in Iberia (12th–16th century), in brick, tile and carpentry (artesonado ceilings). [aka Mudejar style]
K1-6 Seljuk Architecture: the architecture of the Great Seljuqs in Iran and Central Asia (11th–12th century): four-iwan mosques, domed chambers, brick tomb towers and minarets. [aka Great Seljuk architecture]
K1-7 Anatolian Seljuk Architecture: the architecture of the Seljuqs of Rum and the beyliks in Anatolia (12th–14th century): caravanserais, madrasas with carved stone portals, and tomb towers. [aka Rum Seljuk architecture; beylik architecture]
K1-8 Mamluk Architecture: the architecture of the Mamluk sultanate in Egypt and Syria (1250–1517): funerary complexes with domes and minarets, ablaq masonry and carved stone domes. [aka ]
K1-9 Ilkhanid Architecture: the architecture of the Mongol Ilkhanate in Iran (1256–1335): the Mausoleum of Öljeitü at Soltaniyeh and great mosques with tiled portals. [aka ]
K1-10 Timurid Architecture: the architecture of Timur and his successors in Central Asia and Iran (1370–1507): Samarkand, Herat and Mashhad, with bulbous ribbed domes and tile-mosaic revetment. [aka ]
K1-11 Safavid Architecture: the architecture of Safavid Iran (1501–1736), above all Isfahan's Naqsh-e Jahan square, its mosques, palaces and bridges. [aka Isfahani style]
K1-12 Ottoman Architecture: the architecture of the Ottoman empire from the early Bursa and Edirne mosques through the classical domed mosques of Mimar Sinan to Ottoman Baroque. [aka classical Ottoman architecture; Ottoman Baroque]
K1-13 Qajar Architecture: the architecture of Qajar Iran (1789–1925): palaces and mosques with polychrome figural tiles (Golestan Palace). [aka ]
K1-14 Indo-Islamic Architecture: the Islamic architecture of the South Asian sultanates before and beside the Mughals: Delhi Sultanate (Qutb Minar), Gujarat, Bengal, Malwa and the Deccan sultanates. [aka Indo-Saracenic (pre-colonial); Sultanate architecture]
K1-15 Mughal Architecture: the architecture of the Mughal empire (1526–1857): Humayun's tomb, Fatehpur Sikri, the Taj Mahal and the Mughal gardens. [aka Mughal gardens]
K1-16 Sudano-Sahelian Architecture: the earthen architecture of the West African Sahel: mud-brick mosques with projecting torons (Djenné, Timbuktu, Agadez). [aka Sudanese style]
K1-17 Swahili Architecture: the coral-stone architecture of the Swahili coast (Kilwa, Lamu, Zanzibar): mosques, pillar tombs and carved doors. [aka ]
K1-18 Chinese Islamic Architecture: mosques of China in the Chinese timber-frame idiom (Xi'an, Niujie) and the brick and tiled mosques of Xinjiang. [aka Sino-Islamic architecture]
### K2 Historicist & National Revivals
K2-1 Moorish Revival Architecture: the 19th–20th-century revival of Moorish and Islamic forms in Europe and the Americas — synagogues, theatres, baths and palaces. [aka Neo-Moorish; Islamic Revival]
K2-2 Neo-Mudéjar: the Spanish revival of Mudéjar brick and tile (late 19th–early 20th century): bullrings, stations and schools. [aka Neomudéjar]
K2-3 Neo-Mamluk Architecture: the revival of Mamluk forms in Egypt and beyond (late 19th–20th century). [aka Mamluk Revival]
K2-4 First National Architectural Movement: the Turkish national style of 1908–1930 reviving Ottoman and Seljuq forms (Vedat Tek, Mimar Kemaleddin). [aka Turkish Neoclassicism; Neo-Ottoman]

## L Art in Place — made on or into a fixed surface and studied where it stands — rock art, wall painting, in-situ mosaic and tile revetment, geoglyphs — or made on the living body.
### L2 Wall Painting
L2-1 Umayyad Wall Painting: the figural wall paintings of the Umayyad desert palaces, above all the bath of Qusayr 'Amra (8th century). [aka Qusayr 'Amra frescoes]
L2-2 Persian Wall Painting: the palace murals of Safavid and Qajar Iran (Chehel Sotoun, Ali Qapu, Hasht Behesht). [aka Safavid murals; Qajar murals]
### L3 Mosaic & Tile Revetment
L3-1 Umayyad Mosaics: the glass wall mosaics of the Dome of the Rock and the Great Mosque of Damascus (7th–8th century), made by Byzantine-trained craftsmen. [aka ]
L3-2 Zellige: geometric mosaic of hand-cut glazed tiles set in plaster, the wall and floor revetment of Morocco and al-Andalus. [aka zillij; zellij]
L3-3 Persian Tilework: the tile revetment of Iranian and Central Asian architecture: cut-tile mosaic (mo'arraq) of the Timurid and Safavid periods, haft-rangi (seven-colour) tiles and banna'i brick-and-tile. [aka moʿarraq; haft rangi; kashi]
L3-4 Kashi Kari: the tile-mosaic revetment of Mughal and Punjabi buildings (Lahore, Multan, Thatta). [aka kashikari]
L3-5 Aina-kari: mirror mosaic: cut mirror glass set in plaster on walls and ceilings of Iranian palaces and shrines. [aka Persian mirror work; ayeneh-kari]

## Precedence
P1 Manuscript painting vs book arts: Pictures painted in a manuscript or for an album go to A1 Manuscript & Album Painting when the tradition is studied as painting (Persian, Ottoman, Mughal, Deccan, Arab); the book as an object — its script, illumination ornament and binding — goes to C. An illustrated manuscript is placed by what makes it notable: its pictures → A1, its script or illumination → C.
P2 Tiles: ware vs revetment: A tile is placed by its ware in E Ceramics (Iznik tiles under Iznik Pottery; Kashan lustre tiles under Kashan Lustreware). A revetment assembled on site as a named technique — cut-tile mosaic, zellige, kashi kari, mirror mosaic — is L3, because the work is the wall.
P3 Architectural carving: building vs fragment: Carved stucco, stone and wood that survive as museum fragments go to their material family (D1 carving, I1 woodwork). The building that carries them is K; a named in-situ programme of painting or mosaic is L.
P4 Lustre across bodies: Lustre is a surface technique, not a sub-family: a lustreware is placed by its body (earthenware lustre → E1; fritware lustre → E2) and then by its named ware.
P5 Carved hardstone vessels: A vessel carved from rock crystal, jade or other hardstone is D3 (the carving is the work), not glass or metalwork, even when mounted in metal.
P6 Dynasty is a tag: A dynasty, reign, empire or period (Mamluk, Safavid, Qajar, Akbar's reign) is a period tag, never a node. The node is the tradition the work belongs to in its medium: Mamluk enamelled glass (F3), Mamluk metalwork (G1), Mamluk carpets (H1), Mamluk architecture (K).
P7 Place is a tag: A city, production centre, country or culture label (Iznik, Kashan, Tabriz, 'Iranian', 'Syrian') is a place tag. A centre becomes a kind under a leaf only when the field names a distinct school or ware after it (Herat school, Kashan lustreware, Ushak carpets).
P8 Revivals: A revival built outside the original tradition (Moorish Revival synagogues, Neo-Mamluk, the First National Architectural Movement) is a leaf in K Historicist & National Revivals, never a kind of the style it revives.
P9 Makers: A named maker is a kind under the leaf of the tradition they worked in, linked to their school or atelier where one is recorded. A maker active in two traditions is placed where most of their recorded work sits, with a see-also to the other (Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad: Persian schooling, Mughal atelier → Mughal Painting).
P10 Folk and court: A folk or vernacular craft that the field names on its own (suzani, kilim, Berber jewellery) is its own leaf in the medium family; a court or urban tradition of the same medium is a separate leaf. Garment types (abaya, shemagh, ghutra) are not art traditions: they belong to the Possession Taxonomy.
