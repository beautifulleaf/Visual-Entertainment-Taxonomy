In Japan, a {{nihongo|'''pink film'''|ピンク映画|pinku eiga|lead=yes}} is a movie produced by independent studios that includes nudity (hence "pink") or deals with sexual content.<ref>Thomas and Yuko Mihara Weisser. 1998. ''Japanese Cinema Encyclopedia: The Sex Films''. Vital Books.</ref> This encompasses everything from dramas to action thrillers and [[exploitation film]] features.  Many pink films would be analogous to [[erotic thriller]]s such as ''[[Fatal Attraction]]'' or ''[[Basic Instinct]]''.

Independent studios that release pink films include [[OP Eiga]], [[Shintōhō Eiga]], Kokuei, and Xces. The phrase 'pink film' came into use after the major studio [[Toei Company|Toei]] began advertising some of its movies as 'porno' in 1971 and another major studio, [[Nikkatsu]], switched to producing only ''[[Roman Porno]]'' films later that year.<ref>E.g.: {{cite book |first=Jasper |last=Sharp |date=2008 |title=Behind the Pink Curtain: The Complete History of Japanese Sex Cinema |publisher=Fab Press}}</ref>

Until the early 2000s, they were almost exclusively shot on [[35 mm movie film|35 mm film]]. Recently, filmmakers have increasingly used video (while retaining their emphasis on soft-core narrative). Many theaters swapped 35&nbsp;mm for video projectors and began relying on old videos to meet the demand of triple-feature showings.

Films that are now regarded as pink films became wildly popular in the mid-1960s, and made up a large part of the Japanese domestic market through the mid-1980s.<ref>{{cite book |last=Richie|first=Donald |author-link=Donald Richie|title=A Hundred Years of Japanese Film: A Concise History|chapter=After the Wave|year=2001|publisher=Kodansha International|location=Tokyo|isbn=4-7700-2682-X|quote=For a time, almost half of the annual film production figures released in Japan were composed of these hour-long mini-features.}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://194.21.179.166/cecudine/fe_2002/eng/PinkEiga2002.htm|title=Vital flesh: the mysterious world of Pink Eiga|access-date=2007-02-19|last=Domenig|first=Roland|year=2002|quote=Since the mid-1960s, pink eiga have been the biggest Japanese film genre... By the late 1970s the production of pink eiga together with Roman Porno amounted to more than 70% of annual Japanese film production.|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20041118094603/http://194.21.179.166/cecudine/fe_2002/eng/PinkEiga2002.htm|archive-date=2004-11-18}}</ref> In the 1960s, the pink films were largely the product of small, independent studios. Around 1970, major studio Nikkatsu started focusing almost exclusively on erotic content, but Toei, another major film production company, started producing a line of what came to be known as Pinky Violence films. With their access to higher production values and talent, some of these films became critical and popular successes.<ref name="Domenig">{{cite web|url=http://194.21.179.166/cecudine/fe_2002/eng/PinkEiga2002.htm|title=Vital flesh: the mysterious world of Pink Eiga|access-date=2007-02-19|last=Domenig|first=Roland|year=2002|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20041118094603/http://194.21.179.166/cecudine/fe_2002/eng/PinkEiga2002.htm|archive-date=2004-11-18}}</ref> Though the appearance of the [[chronology of adult videos in Japan|adult video]] led viewers to move away from pink film in the 1980s, films in this genre are still being produced.

==Description==

The "pink film", or "eroduction" (erotic production) as it was first called,<ref>Domenig. ''Vital Flesh''. "The term pink eiga was first coined in 1963 by journalist Murai Minoru. But it did not come into general use until the late 1960s. In the early years the films were known as 'eroduction films' (erodakushon eiga) or 'three-million-yen-films' (sanbyakuman eiga)."</ref> is a cinematic genre that's largely gone unrepresented in the West.<ref name="Domenig"/> Though called pornography, the terms "[[erotica]]", "[[soft porn]]" and "[[sexploitation]]" have been suggested as more appropriate, although none of these precisely matches the pink film genre.<ref name="Weisser-20">{{cite book |last1=Weisser|first1=Thomas|author2=Yuko Mihara Weisser |title=Japanese Cinema Encyclopedia: The Sex Films|url=https://archive.org/details/japanesecinemaen00weis|url-access=limited|year=1998|publisher=Vital Books: Asian Cult Cinema Publications|location=Miami|isbn=1-889288-52-7|page=[https://archive.org/details/japanesecinemaen00weis/page/n21 20]}}</ref>

The Japanese film ethics board Eirin has long enforced a ban on the display of genitals and pubic hair. This restriction forced Japanese filmmakers to develop sometimes elaborate means of avoiding showing the "working parts", as American film historian [[Donald Richie]] puts it.<ref name="Richie-156"/> He wrote:
{{blockquote|The eroductions are the limpest of softcore, and though there is much breast and buttock display, though there are simulations of intercourse, none of the working parts are ever shown. Indeed, one pubic hair breaks an unwritten but closely observed code. Though this last problem is solved by shaving the actresses, the larger remains: how to stimulate when the means are missing.<ref name="Richie-156">{{cite book |last=Richie|first=Donald|title=A Lateral View: Essays on Culture and Style in Contemporary Japan|url=https://archive.org/details/lateralviewessay00rich|url-access=limited|orig-year=1972|year=1987|publisher=Stone Bridge Press|location=Berkeley, California|isbn=0-9628137-4-5|chapter=The Japanese Eroduction|page=[https://archive.org/details/lateralviewessay00rich/page/156 156]}}</ref>}}

To work around this censorship, most Japanese directors positioned props—lamps, candles, bottles, etc.—at strategic locations to block the banned body parts. When this was not done, the most common alternative techniques are digital scrambling, covering the prohibited area with a black box or a fuzzy white spot, known as a [[pixelization|mosaic]] or "[[Fogging (censorship)|fogging]]".<ref>Weisser. p. 23.</ref>

Some have claimed that it is this [[censorship]] that gives the Japanese erotic cinema its particular style. Richie wrote:
{{blockquote|American pornography is kept forever on its elemental level because, showing all, it need do nothing else; Japanese eroductions have to do something else since they cannot show all. The stultified impulse has created some extraordinary works of art, a few films among them. None of these, however, are found among eroductions.<ref>Richie. ''The Japanese Eroduction''. p. 163.</ref>}}

Richie makes a distinction between the erotic films of the major studios such as Nikkatsu and Toei as against the low-budget pink films produced by independents such as OP Eiga.

Contrasting the pink film with Western [[pornographic film]]s, Pia Harritz says, "What really stands out is the ability of pinku eiga to engage the spectator in more than just scenes with close-ups of genitals and finally the complexity in the representation of gender and the human mind."<ref name="Harritz">{{cite web|last=Harritz |first=Pia D. |year=2006 |title=Consuming the Female Body: Pinku Eiga and the case of Sagawa Issei |url=http://www.medievidenskab-odense.dk/index.php?id=57 |work=mediavidenskab |access-date=2007-07-07 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070702120500/http://www.medievidenskab-odense.dk/index.php?id=57 |archive-date=2007-07-02 |url-status=dead }}</ref>

Richie and Harritz enumerate the fundamental elements of the pink film formula as:
# The film must have a required minimum quota of sex scenes.<ref>Richie. ''The Japanese Eroduction'' pp. 159–160. "In theory, directors are instructed to aim at some kind of sex scene every five minutes; in practice, however, it has proved almost impossible to construct a story-line which allows this, with the results that sex scenes are sometimes fewer but somewhat longer."</ref>
# The film must be approximately one hour in duration.<ref>Domenig. ''Vital Flesh''. "Pink eiga... typically 60 minutes long..."</ref>
# It must be filmed on 16&nbsp;mm or 35&nbsp;mm film within one week.<ref>Richie. ''The Japanese Eroduction'' p. 157. "The shooting-time for each remains short—a week at the most..."</ref>
# The film must be made on a very limited budget.<ref>Harritz. Writing in 2006, Pia Harritz gives the required budget as about $35,000.</ref>

== History ==

=== Background ===
In the years since the end of [[World War II]], eroticism had been gradually making its way into Japanese cinema. The first kiss to be seen in Japanese film—discreetly half-hidden by an umbrella—caused a national sensation in 1946.<ref>{{cite book |last=Bornoff|first=Nicholas|title=Pink Samurai: An Erotic Exploration of Japanese Society; The Pursuit and Politics of Sex in Japan|orig-year=1991|edition=Paperback|year=1994|publisher=HarperCollins|location=London|isbn=0-586-20576-4|chapter=18 (Naked Dissent)|page=602}}</ref> Although throughout the 1940s and early 1950s nudity in Japanese movie theaters, as in most of the world, was a taboo,<ref name="Weisser-22">Weisser, p. 22.</ref> some films from the mid-1950s such as [[Shintoho]]'s female pearl-diver films starring buxom [[Michiko Maeda]], began showing more flesh than would have previously been imaginable in the Japanese cinema.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Anderson|first1=Joseph |last2=Richie |first2=Donald |title=The Japanese Film: Art and Industry|edition=Expanded|year=1982|publisher=Princeton University Press|location=Princeton, New Jersey|pages=266–267}}</ref> During the same period, the ''[[taiyozoku]]'' films on the teen-age "Sun Tribe", such as [[Kō Nakahira]]'s ''[[Crazed Fruit]]'' (1956), introduced unprecedented sexual frankness into Japanese films.<ref>{{cite book |last=Sato|first=Tadao |author-link=Tadao Sato|translator-first=Gregory |translator-last=Barrett|title=Currents in Japanese Cinema|orig-year=1982|edition=paperback |year=1987|publisher=Kodansha |location=Tokyo|pages=212–213|isbn=0-87011-815-3}}</ref>

Foreign films of this time, such as [[Ingmar Bergman]]'s ''[[Summer with Monika]]'' (1953), [[Louis Malle]]'s ''[[Les Amants|Amants]]'' (1958), and [[Russ Meyer]]'s ''[[The Immoral Mr. Teas|Immoral Mr. Teas]]'' (1959) introduced female nudity into international cinema, and were imported to Japan without problem.<ref name="Weisser-22"/> Nevertheless, until the early 1960s, graphic depictions of nudity and sex in Japanese film could only be seen in single-reel "stag films," made by film producers such as those depicted in [[Shohei Imamura|Imamura]]'s film ''[[The Pornographers]]'' (1966).<ref name="Erotic Nightmares">{{cite web |url=http://www.midnighteye.com/features/focus_takechi.shtml|first=Jasper|last=Sharp|title=Tetsuji Takechi: Erotic Nightmares|access-date=2007-03-15|work=www.midnighteye.com}}</ref>

===First wave (The "Age of Competition" 1962–1971)===
The first wave of the pink film in Japan was contemporary with the similar American [[Sexploitation|sexploitation film]] genres, the "nudie-cuties" and "roughies".<ref>{{cite journal |last=Fentone|first=Steve|year=1998|title=A Rip of the Flesh: The Japanese 'Pink Film' Cycle|journal=She|volume=2|issue=11|page=5}}</ref> Nudity and sex officially entered Japanese cinema with [[Satoru Kobayashi (director)|Satoru Kobayashi]]'s controversial and popular independent production ''[[Flesh Market]]'' (''Nikutai no Ichiba'', 1962), which is considered the first true pink film.<ref name="Weisser-21">Weisser, p. 21.</ref> Made for 8 million yen, Kobayashi's independent feature film took in over 100 million yen. Kobayashi remained active in directing pink films until the 1990s. [[Tamaki Katori]], the star of the film, went on to become one of the leading early pink film stars, appearing in over 600, and earning the title "Pink Princess".<ref name="Connell">{{cite news|first=Ryann|last=Connell|title=Japan's former Pink Princess trades raunchy scenes for rural canteen|url=http://mdn.mainichi-msn.co.jp/waiwai/archive/news/2006/03/20060302p2g00m0dm014000c.html|work=[[Mainichi Shimbun]]|date=March 2, 2006|access-date=2007-03-06}}{{dead link|date=October 2017|bot=medic}}{{cbignore|bot=medic}}</ref>

In 1964, maverick [[kabuki]], theater and film director [[Tetsuji Takechi]] directed ''[[Daydream (1964 film)|Daydream]]'', a big-budget film distributed by the major studio Shochiku. Takechi's ''Black Snow'' (1965), resulted in the director's arrest on charges of obscenity and a high-profile trial, which became a major battle between Japan's intellectuals and the establishment. Takechi won the lawsuit, and the publicity surrounding the trial helped bring about a boom in the production of pink films.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.allmovie.com/cg/avg.dll?p=avg&sql=1:249642|archive-url=https://archive.today/20120719131852/http://www.allmovie.com/cg/avg.dll?p=avg&sql=1:249642|url-status=dead|archive-date=2012-07-19|title=''Kuroi Yuki''|access-date=2007-10-29|last=Firsching|first=Robert|publisher=[[Allmovie]]|quote=The resultant obscenity trial... ended with a landmark decision which allowed complete narrative freedom in Japanese films. This development paved the way for the thousands of softcore pinku eiga and S & M films which would define Japanese exploitation cinema until... the late '80s...}}</ref>

In her introduction to the Weisser's ''Japanese Cinema Encyclopedia: The Sex Films'', actress [[Naomi Tani]] calls this period in pink film production "The Age of Competition".<ref>Weisser, p. 12.</ref> Though Japan's major studios, such as [[Nikkatsu]] and [[Shochiku]] made occasional forays into erotica in the 1960s, such as director [[Seijun Suzuki]]'s ''[[Gate of Flesh]]'' (1964)—the first Japanese film from one of the big four major studios to contain nudity<ref name="Weisser-21"/>—the majority of erotic films were made by the independents. Independent studios such as Nihon Cinema and World Eiga made dozens of cheap, profitable "eroductions". Among the most influential independent studios producing pink films in this era were Shintōhō Eiga (the second studio to use this name), [[Million Film]], Kantō, and [[OP Eiga|Ōkura]].<ref name="Weisser-20"/> Typically shown on a three-film program, these films were made by these companies to show at their own chain of specialty theaters.<ref>Richie. ''A Hundred Years of Japanese Film''</ref>

Another major pink film studio, Wakamatsu Studios, was formed by director [[Kōji Wakamatsu]] in 1965, after quitting Nikkatsu. Known as "The Pink Godfather",<ref>Weisser, p. 287.</ref> and called "the most important director to emerge in the pink film genre",<ref>{{cite book|last=Desser|first=David|author-link=David Desser|title=Eros Plus Massacre: An Introduction to the Japanese New Wave Cinema|year=1988|publisher=Indiana University Press|location=Bloomington and Indianapolis|isbn=0-253-31961-7|pages=[https://archive.org/details/erosplusmassacre00davi/page/99 99–101]|url-access=registration|url=https://archive.org/details/erosplusmassacre00davi/page/99}}</ref> Wakamatsu's independent productions are critically respected works usually concerned with sex and extreme violence mixed with political messages.<ref>{{cite web |last1=Iwauchi |first1=Hideki |author2=Koji Wakamatsu (interviewee) |url=http://www.insite-tokyo.com/interview/15wakamatsu/index.html |title=Koji Wakamatsu Film Director Interview |access-date=2007-07-07 |work=www.insite-tokyo.com |quote=He... produced numerous films that are shocking for their treatments of sex, violence, and politics. |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070426032143/http://www.insite-tokyo.com/interview/15wakamatsu/index.html |archive-date=2007-04-26 |url-status=dead }}</ref> His most controversial early films dealing with [[misogyny]] and sadism are ''[[The Embryo Hunts In Secret]]'' (1966), ''[[Violated Angels]]'' (1967), and ''[[Go, Go, Second Time Virgin]]'' (1969).

Three other important pink film directors of this time, [[Kan Mukai]], [[:ja:小川欽也|Kin'ya Ogawa]] and [[:ja:山本晋也|Shin'ya Yamamoto]] are known as "The Heroes of the First Wave".<ref>Weisser, p. 105.</ref> In 1965, the same year as Wakamatsu became independent, directors Kan Mukai and [[Giichi Nishihara]] established their own production companies, Mukai Productions and Aoi Eiga.<ref>{{cite book |last=Sharp|first=Jasper|title=Behind the Pink Curtain: The Complete History of Japanese Sex Cinema|page=53 |year=2008|publisher=FAB Press|location=Guildford|isbn=978-1-903254-54-7}}</ref>

The "first queen of Japanese sex movies" was [[Noriko Tatsumi]],<ref>Weisser, p. 81.</ref> who made films at World Eiga and Nihon Cinema with director [[Kōji Seki]].<ref>Weisser, p. 79.</ref> Other major Sex Queens of the first wave of pink film included Setsuko Ogawa,<ref>Weisser, p. 131.</ref> Mari Iwai,<ref>Weisser, p. 441.</ref> Keiko Kayama,<ref>Weisser, p. 151.</ref> and Miki Hayashi.<ref>Weisser, p. 153.</ref> Other pink film stars of the era include Tamaki Katori, who appeared in many films for Giichi Nishihara and Kōji Wakamatsu; Kemi Ichiboshi, whose specialty was playing the role of a violated innocent;<ref>Weisser, p. 103.</ref> and Mari Nagisa.<ref>Weisser, p. 197.</ref> Younger starlets like [[Naomi Tani]], and [[Kazuko Shirakawa]] were starting their careers and already making names for themselves in the pink film industry, but are best remembered today for their work with Nikkatsu during the 1970s.

===Toei pornography===

Most exploitation films produced in the 1960s were made by low-budget independent companies.  The major studio [[Toei Company|Toei]]  released a few films with female nudity, starting with ''[[Kunoichi ninpō]]'' in 1964 by director [[Sadao Nakajima]].  In films like his ero-guro series and ''[[Shogun's Joys of Torture|Joys of Torture]]'' series of the late 1960s director [[Teruo Ishii]] provided a model for Toei's sexploitation ventures by "establishing a queasy mix of comedy and torture."<ref>Macias, p. 189.</ref>

In 1972, Richie reported, "In Japan, the eroduction is the only type of picture that retains an assured patronage."<ref>Richie. ''The Japanese Eroduction'', p. 158.</ref>  To tap into this market, Toei began advertising some of its films using the English word 'porno,' a new word at the time.  ''Onsen mimizu geisha'' directed by [[Norifumi Suzuki]] and starring [[Reiko Ike]] was the first such in July 1971.

Producer Kanji Amao designed a group of series—{{Lang|ja-Latn|shigeki rosen}} (Sensational Line), {{Lang|ja-Latn|ijoseiai rosen}} (Abnormal Line), and {{Lang|ja-Latn|harenchi rosen}} (Shameless Line), today referred to by English critics as Toei's "Pinky Violence".<ref name="Domenig"/><ref>Macias, p. 189</ref> Most of Toei's films in this style used eroticism in conjunction with violent and action-filled stories. Several of these films have the theme of strong women exacting violent revenge for past injustices. The series was launched with the ''Delinquent Girl Boss'' (''Zubeko Bancho'') films starring [[Reiko Oshida]].<ref>{{cite book |last=D.|first=Chris|author-link=Chris D.|title=Toei's Bad Girl Cinema (booklet to DVD set The Pinky Violence Collection|year=2005|publisher=Panik House Entertainment|page=8}}</ref> Other series in the ''Pinky Violence'' genre included [[Norifumi Suzuki]]'s ''Girl Boss'' (''[[Sukeban]]'') films, and the ''[[Terrifying Girls' High School]]'' films, both starring [[Reiko Ike]] and [[Miki Sugimoto]].<ref>Macias, p. 190</ref><ref>Chris D., p. 10.</ref>

Other examples of Toei's films in this genre include [[Shunya Ito]]'s {{Lang|ja-Latn|Sasori}} (Scorpion) series of [[women in prison films]] based on [[Toru Shinohara]]'s [[manga]]. Starting with ''[[Female Prisoner 701: Scorpion|Female Prisoner #701: Scorpion]]'' (1972), the ''Scorpion'' series starred [[Meiko Kaji]], who had left Nikkatsu Studios to distance herself from their ''Roman Porno'' series. Toei also set the standard for Japanese [[nunsploitation]] films (a subgenre imported from Italy) with the critically acclaimed ''[[School of the Holy Beast]]'' (1974) directed by [[Norifumi Suzuki]]. Toei also produced a whole series of erotic samurai pictures such as ''Bohachi Bushido: Clan of the Forgotten Eight'' (Bōhachi Bushidō: Poruno Jidaigeki) (1973).

===Nikkatsu ''Roman Porno''===
{{see also|List of Nikkatsu Roman Porno films{{!}}List of Nikkatsu ''Roman Porno'' films|Category:Nikkatsu Roman Porno{{!}}Category:Nikkatsu ''Roman Porno''}}

In 1971, Takashi Itamochi, president of Nikkatsu, Japan's oldest major film studio, decided to stop his own company's involvement with action films and start making sexploitation films.<ref>{{cite book |last=Macias |first=Patrick |title=TokyoScope: The Japanese Cult Film Companion |year=2001 |publisher=Cadence Books|location=San Francisco|isbn=1-56931-681-3|chapter=Nikkatsu's Roman Porno|page=187}}</ref> Like Toei, Nikkatsu had made some previous films in the sexploitation market, such as ''Story of Heresy in Meiji Era'' (1968) and ''Tokyo Bathhouse'' (1968), which featured over 30 sex-film stars in cameo appearances.<ref>Weisser, pp. 420, 434.</ref> Nikkatsu launched its [[List of Nikkatsu Roman Porno films|''Roman Porno'' series]] in November 1971 with ''[[Apartment Wife: Affair In the Afternoon]]'', starring [[Kazuko Shirakawa]].<ref>Sato. p. 244.</ref> The film became a huge hit, inspired 20 sequels within seven years, established Shirakawa as Nikkatsu's first "Queen", and successfully launched the high-profile ''Roman Porno'' series. Director [[Masaru Konuma]] says that the process of making ''Roman Porno'' was the same as that of making a pink film except for the higher budget.<ref>[[Masaru Konuma|Konuma, Masaru]]. Interviewed by Weisser, Thomas and Yuko Mihara Weisser. (1998). "An Interview with Masaru Konuma; An exclusive ACC interview with Nikkatsu's most notorious director conducted... in Tokyo on November 6, 1998." in ''Asian Cult Cinema'', #22, 1st Quarter 1999, p. 21."The company wanted to convince people that these movies were somehow different, perhaps to make them immediately socially acceptable. However—from the creator's side—there was no difference between the making of Roman Porn and Pink."</ref> Nikkatsu made these higher-quality sexploitation films almost exclusively, at an average rate of three per month,<ref>{{cite book |last=Bornoff|first=Nicholas|title=Pink Samurai: An Erotic Exploration of Japanese Society; The Pursuit and Politics of Sex in Japan|orig-year=1991|edition=Paperback|year=1994 |publisher=HarperCollins |location=London|isbn=0-586-20576-4|chapter=Bye-Bye Pink Cinema, Hello Adult Video|page=603}}</ref> for the next 17 years.

Nikkatsu gave its ''Roman Porno'' directors a great deal of artistic freedom in creating their films, as long as they met the official minimum quota of four nude or sex scenes per hour.<ref name="Weisser-204">Weisser, p. 204</ref> The result was a series that was popular both with audiences and with critics.<ref>Macias, p. 187. "While single men and curious couples alike lined up for ''roman porno'' fare, film critics had no hesitations about singing their praises."</ref> One or two ''Roman Pornos'' appeared on the top-ten lists of Japanese critics every year throughout the run of the series.<ref>{{cite book|last=Hirano|first=Kyoko|editor=William Luhr|title=World Cinema Since 1945|year=1987|publisher=The Ungar Publishing Company|location=New York|isbn=0-8044-3078-0|chapter=Japan|quote=at least one or two ''Roman Pornos'' have been chosen every year since 1971 as among the ten best films of the year by Japanese critics.|page=[https://archive.org/details/worldcinemasince00luhr/page/412 412]|chapter-url-access=registration|chapter-url=https://archive.org/details/worldcinemasince00luhr/page/412}}</ref> Nikkatsu's higher-quality sex films essentially took the pink film market away from the smaller, independent studios until the mid-1980s, when [[Chronology of adult videos in Japan|adult videos]] began to lure away much of the pink film's clientele.<ref name="Weisser-20"/>

[[Tatsumi Kumashiro]] was one of the major directors of the ''Roman Porno''. Kumashiro directed a string of financial and critical hits unprecedented in Japanese cinematic history, including ''[[Ichijo's Wet Desire]]'' (1972) and ''[[Woman with Red Hair (film)|Woman with Red Hair]]'' (1979), starring [[Junko Miyashita]].<ref name="Weisser-495">Weisser, p. 495.</ref> He became known as the "King of Nikkatsu ''Roman porno''"<ref name="Weisser-204"/><ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.filmquarterly.org/pdfs/article5701.pdf |title=A New View of Porn: The Films of Tatsumi Kumashiro |access-date=2007-03-06 |last=Johnson |first=William |year=2003 |work=[[Film Quarterly]], vol. 57, no.1, Fall 2003 |publisher=University of California Press |page=12 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070304111719/http://www.filmquarterly.org/pdfs/article5701.pdf |archive-date=2007-03-04 |url-status=dead }}</ref> [[Noboru Tanaka]], director of ''[[A Woman Called Sada Abe]]'' (1975), is judged by many critics today to have been the best of Nikkatsu's ''Roman Porno'' directors.<ref>Weisser, pp. 323, 359.</ref><ref>{{cite book |last=Thompson|first=Bill|editor=Frank N. Magill|title=Magill's Survey of Cinema: Foreign Language Films; Volume 4|year=1985|publisher=Salem Press|location=Englewood Cliffs, N.J.|isbn=0-89356-247-5|pages=1568–1573|chapter=''Jitsuroko [sic] Abe Sada''}}</ref> The [[Sadomasochism|S&M]] subgenre of the ''Roman Porno'' was established in 1974 when the studio hired [[Naomi Tani]] to star in ''[[Flower and Snake (1974 film)|Flower and Snake]]'' (based on an [[Oniroku Dan]] novel), and ''[[Wife to be Sacrificed]]'', both directed by Masaru Konuma.<ref>Konuma, p. 22–23.</ref> Tani's immense popularity established her as Nikkatsu's third ''Roman Porno'' Queen, and the first of their S&M Queens.<ref>Weisser, p. 329.</ref>
Tani was also directed by the important director [[Shôgorô Nishimura]] in titles that became classics, such as ''[[Rope Cosmetology]]'' (1978).
Other subgenres developed under the ''Roman Porno'' line included "Violent Pink", established in 1976 by director [[Yasuharu Hasebe]].<ref>Hasebe, Yasuharu. (1998). Interviewed by Thomas and Yuko Mihara Weisser in Tokyo, 1999, in ''Asian Cult Cinema'', #25, 4th Quarter, 1999, p. 39.</ref>

===1980s===
When ownership of [[VCR]]s first became widespread in the early 1980s, adult videos made their appearance, and quickly became highly popular.<ref>{{cite web|last=Schönherr|first=Johannes|url=http://www.midnighteye.com/features/company-matsuo-and-the-world-of-japanese-adult-video/|title=Midnight Eye feature: Company Matsuo and the World of Japanese Adult Video|date=29 December 2006|website=[[Midnight Eye]]|access-date=21 July 2019}}</ref> As early as 1982, the AVs had already attained an approximately equal share of the adult entertainment market with theatrical erotic films.<ref name="Weisser-29">Weisser, p. 29.</ref> In 1984, new government censorship policies and an agreement between [[Eirin]] (the Japanese film-rating board) and the pink-film companies added to Nikkatsu's difficulties by putting drastic new restrictions on theatrical films. Theatrical pink movie profits dropped 36% within a month of the new ruling.<ref name="Weisser-29"/> Eirin dealt a serious blow to the pink film industry in 1988 by introducing stricter requirements for sex-related theatrical films. Nikkatsu responded by discontinuing their Roman Porno line. ''Bed Partner'' (1988) was the final film of the venerable 17-year-old ''Roman Porno'' series. Nikkatsu continued to distribute films under the name Ropponica, and pink films through Excess Films, however these were not nearly as popular or critically respected as the Roman Porno series had been in its heyday.<ref>Weisser, pp. 30, 63, 155.</ref> By the end of the 1980s, adult videos had become the main form of adult cinematic entertainment in Japan.

The dominant directors of pink films of the 1980s, Genji Nakamura, [[Banmei Takahashi]] and [[Mamoru Watanabe]] are known collectively as "The Three Pillars Of Pink".<ref>Weisser, p. 231.</ref> All three were veterans of the pink film industry since the 1960s. Coming to prominence in the 1980s, a time when the theatrical porn film was facing considerable difficulties on several fronts, this group is known for elevating the pink film above its low origins by concentrating on technical finesse and narrative content. Some critics dubbed the style of their films "''pink'' art".<ref name="Weisser-495"/>

By the time Nakamura joined Nikkatsu in 1983, he had already directed over 100 films.<ref>Weisser, p. 60</ref> While the plots of his films, which could be extremely misogynistic, were not highly respected, his visual style earned him a reputation for "erotic sensitivity."<ref name="Weisser-495"/> Nakamura directed one of Japan's first widely distributed, well-received films with a homosexual theme, ''[[Beautiful Mystery|Beautiful Mystery: Legend of the Big Penis]]'' (1983),<ref>Weisser, p. 229</ref> for Nikkatsu's ENK Productions, which was founded in 1983 to focus on gay-themed pink films.<ref name="Domenig"/> Some of Nakamura's later pink films were directed in collaboration with [[Ryūichi Hiroki]], and Hitoshi Ishikawa under the group pseudonym Go Ijuin.<ref>Weisser, p. 383.</ref>

Banmei Takahashi directed "intricate, highly stylistic ''pinku eiga''",<ref>Weisser, p. 183.</ref> including ''New World of Love'' (1994), the first Japanese theatrical film to display genitals.<ref>Weisser, p. 292.</ref> Another prominent cult director of this era, [[Kazuo Komizu|Kazuo "Gaira" Komizu]], is known for his [[Herschell Gordon Lewis]]-influenced "splatter-eros" films, which bridge the genres of horror and erotica.<ref>Weisser, pp. 126–127.</ref>

===1990s===
Nikkatsu, Japan's largest producer of pink films during the 1970s and 1980s, filed for bankruptcy protection in 1993.<ref>Macias, p. 188.</ref> Nevertheless, even in this most difficult period for the pink film, the genre never completely died out, and continued exploring new artistic realms. Indeed, at this time the pink film was viewed as one of the last refuges of the "[[Auteur theory|auteur]]" in Japan. So long as the director provided the requisite number of sex scenes, he was free to explore his own thematic and artistic interests.<ref>Zeze, Takahisa quoted in {{cite web |url=http://www.midnighteye.com/interviews/takahisa_zeze.shtml|title=Takahisa Zeze interview|access-date=2007-10-04|work=midnighteye.com |quote=Question: "Pink film is often seen as one of the last few reserves of the auteur. It is often said that as long as you deliver so many sex scenes in one hour, the director can fill the rest of the running time with whatever he wants. Is this true?" Zeze: "Yes it is. We believed it was true at the time, so we tried to make what we wanted to make..."}}</ref>

Three of the most prominent pink film directors of the 1990s, [[Kazuhiro Sano]], [[Toshiki Satō]] and [[Takahisa Zeze]] all made their directorial debuts in 1989. A fourth, [[Hisayasu Satō]], debuted in 1985. Coming to prominence during one of the most precarious times for the pink film, these directors worked under the assumption that each film could be their last, and so largely ignored their audience to concentrate on intensely personal, experimental themes. These directors even broke one of the fundamental ''pink'' rules by cutting down in the sex scenes in pursuit of their own artistic concerns. Their films were considered "difficult"—dark, complex, and largely unpopular with the older ''pink'' audience. The title
{{nihongo|"Four Heavenly Kings of Pink"|ピンク四天王|pinku shitennō}} was applied to these directors, at first sarcastically, by disgruntled theater owners. On the other hand, Roland Domenig, in his essay on the pink film, says that their work offers "a refreshing contrast to the formulaic and stereotyped films that make up the larger part of ''pink eiga'' production, and are strongly influenced by the notion of the filmmaker as auteur."<ref name="Domenig"/>

===21st century===
The most prominent group of seven pink film directors all began as assistant directors to the ''shitennō''. Their films display individualistic styles and introspective character indicative of the insecurity of Japan's post-bubble generation. Known together as the {{nihongo|"Seven Lucky Gods of Pink"|ピンク七福神|pinku shichifukujin}} they are [[Toshiya Ueno]], [[Shinji Imaoka]], Yoshitaka Kamata, Toshiro Enomoto, [[Yūji Tajiri]], [[Mitsuru Meike]] and [[Rei Sakamoto]].<ref name="Domenig"/> Ueno was the first director of this group to rise to prominence, acting as an "advance guard" for the group when his ''[[Keep on Masturbating: Non-Stop Pleasure]]'' (1994) won the "Best Film" award at the [[Pink Grand Prix]].<ref>Sharp, ''Behind the Pink Curtain'', p. 311.</ref> Founded in 1989,<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.uplink.co.jp/pinkribbon/bangai.html#3|title=3.
